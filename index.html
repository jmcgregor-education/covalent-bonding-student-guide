Why does water behave the way it does?

Water (H2O) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) are both covalent compounds made of the same two elements. Yet one is essential for life and the other is used as a disinfectant and bleaching agent. Sugar dissolves in water, but oil does not, even though both are covalent. Before we dig in, jot down:

  • I notice: two things you observe about water, oil, or sugar.
  • I wonder: one question you have about why they behave differently.

Keep your questions. We will come back to them at the end.

Section 1 · Sharing Electrons

What is a covalent bond?

Ionic compounds form when electrons are transferred from one atom to another. Covalent compounds form between nonmetals (and metalloids) that share electrons instead. Sharing lets each atom reach a stable outer shell, usually eight valence electrons, which is the octet rule.

Covalent bondA bond formed when atoms share electrons.
MoleculeA group of atoms held together by covalent bonds.
Covalent compoundA compound of two or more nonmetals that share electrons. Also called a molecular compound.
Valence electronsElectrons in the outermost energy level that take part in bonding.
Octet ruleAtoms tend to share electrons to reach eight valence electrons.
Molecular formulaShows the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule.

Key ideas

  • Covalent compounds can form in more than one ratio between the same two nonmetals (CO and CO2 are both carbon and oxygen). Ionic compounds have one fixed ratio.
  • A molecular formula is never reduced. H2O2 is a real molecule, not a simplified version of HO.
  • Hydrogen forms only one bond, and it is never the central atom in a molecule.
  • Carbon usually forms four bonds (any mix of single, double, or triple) and rarely has lone pairs.

Section 2 · Polar or Nonpolar?

How evenly are electrons shared?

Electronegativity is how strongly an atom pulls on shared electrons. The bigger the difference between two atoms, the more unevenly the electrons are shared. Use these guidelines:

Electronegativity difference (ΔEN)Bond type
Less than 0.4Nonpolar covalent: electrons shared about equally
0.4 to 1.7Polar covalent: electrons shared unevenly
Greater than 1.7Usually ionic: a rough guideline (see challenge below)

Polarity and solubility

"Like dissolves like." Water is a polar molecule: its hydrogen end is slightly positive and its oxygen end is slightly negative. That lets water form attractions with other polar molecules and ions, so sugar (full of polar O–H groups) dissolves. Oils and waxes are mostly nonpolar, so water cannot attract them well and they stay separate.

Section 3 · Naming & Formulas

Naming covalent compounds with Greek prefixes

Covalent compounds use Greek prefixes to say how many atoms of each element are present, because the same two elements can combine in several ratios.

Number12345678910
Prefixmono-di-tri-tetra-penta-hexa-hepta-octa-nona-deca-
  1. Name the first element with its prefix. Leave off mono- here. (CO is carbon monoxide, not monocarbon monoxide.)
  2. Name the second element with its prefix (use mono- if needed), then change the ending to -ide.
  3. If the element name starts with a vowel, drop the final a or o of the prefix (but never the i). Example: tetra + oxide becomes tetroxide.
  4. To write the formula, the prefixes become the subscripts. No prefix means one atom.
Careful: Ionic compounds never use prefixes. Covalent compounds always do (except that the first element skips mono-).

Section 4 · Lewis Structures

Drawing electron dot structures

A Lewis structure shows how atoms share electrons. A dash between two symbols is one shared pair. Dots beside a symbol are lone pairs (nonbonding) or single unpaired electrons (ready to bond).

Bond capacity cheat sheet

H11Never
Halogens (F, Cl, Br, I)71Rarely

Method: Needed · Available · Shared

  1. Needed: Add up the electrons each atom needs for a full shell (8 for most nonmetals, 2 for hydrogen).
  2. Available: Add up the valence electrons all the atoms actually have.
  3. Shared: Needed minus Available. Divide by 2 to get the number of bonds.
  4. Put the least electronegative element in the center (never hydrogen). Connect the outer atoms with single bonds.
  5. Use any leftover bonds as double or triple bonds between atoms that can form them.
  6. Add lone pairs to finish each atom's shell. Check that the total electrons match your Available count.

Each dash is a shared pair. Dots are lone pairs. Notice how the number of bonds matches each atom's bond capacity.

Section 5 · VSEPR Shapes

Predicting molecular shape

VSEPR (valence shell electron pair repulsion) says electron groups around a central atom spread out as far apart as possible. A group is a bonded atom or a lone pair. Each bond counts as one group, even a double or triple bond. Lone pairs take up more space than bonded atoms, which squeezes the bond angles.

Watch Out

Common mistakes

  • "Molecular formulas should be simplified." No. H2O2 and HO are different substances.
  • "Lone pairs don't change the shape." They do. They push bonded atoms closer together and change the angle and the name of the shape.
  • "Carbon can have lone pairs or skip its octet." Carbon almost always forms four bonds with no lone pairs.
  • "Drawing the Lewis structure is extra work." The Lewis structure shows where the electron groups are, which you need before you can predict the shape.
  • "Ionic compounds use prefixes too." Only covalent compounds use Greek prefixes.
Language check: Atoms do not "want" electrons or get "greedy." Those are analogies. Bonding happens because sharing electrons lowers the energy of the atoms. Where do you think the "relationship" analogy breaks down?
